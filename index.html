About

I'm an environmental economist working on agricultural policy, land use and climate resilience.

I'm a part-time DPhil candidate in Geography and the Environment at the University of Oxford, where my thesis studies how agricultural support policies trade off production, the environment and rural welfare. My work uses natural experiments, remote sensing and administrative and survey data.

I'm also co-founder of Mode Economics, a climate adaptation and resilience economics advisory, where I've worked on climate finance, public financial management, environmental insurance policy and fiscal resilience in the Pacific. Before that I was an economist at Vivid Economics and McKinsey & Company, held a World Economic Forum fellowship, and worked on environmental policy at the National Audubon Society.

I hold an MSc in Environmental Economics and Climate Change from the London School of Economics, and an MA and BA in Economics from the University of Montana.

Research

Works in Progress

Who Gains from Maize Support? Trade-offs between Producers, Consumers and Forests (DPhil Paper 1)

Agricultural support is usually judged on a single outcome, such as output or farm income. This paper compares the gains and losses across groups at once: producers, consumers further down the supply chain, and the environment. Using maize support as the setting, it estimates how much of the support passes through to consumer prices, how it shifts producer planting and land-use decisions, and what that implies for forest loss.

Subsidies as Insurance: Wealth and Risk Effects of Producer Support (DPhil Paper 2)

Producer support can raise farmers' expected income, reduce the variance of that income, or both. This paper separates the two channels to test how far subsidies act as an insurance mechanism, and compares them with crop insurance, asking whether insurance drives the same environmental pressure as other forms of support.

Grain Reserves and Resilience to Weather Shocks (DPhil Paper 3)

This paper studies whether public food stocks and related policies cushion consumers when severe weather disrupts production and transport, and how those buffers compare with other support types in building resilience.

Completed Research

Restoring Natural Fire and the Cost of Suppression in American Wilderness: Evidence from a Policy Experiment (MSc dissertation, LSE, 2019)

A century of total fire suppression in the American West left forests overgrown and fires more severe and costly. Under wildland fire use (WFU), naturally ignited fires in Wilderness Areas are monitored and allowed to burn as fuel treatment rather than put out. This dissertation estimates the effect of that policy on the suppression cost of later fires that overlap previously burned areas, using ignition cause and daily weather conditions as instrumental variables for treatment. Planned treatment under WFU reduced total and per-hectare suppression costs, though not significantly, while unplanned wildfire produced significant cost reductions, suggesting WFU applied at larger scale could do the same. Effects on fire intensity and fuel availability were inconclusive.

The Role of Behaviour in the Effectiveness of Indoor Air Pollution Interventions (MA thesis, University of Montana, 2017)

Using panel data from a randomized placebo-controlled trial of wood stove changeouts and air filters, this thesis tests whether each intervention improved indoor air quality and whether households changed their behaviour in ways that offset it. Air filters improved indoor air quality while cleaner stoves did not, and behavioural responses did not differ across treatment groups.
